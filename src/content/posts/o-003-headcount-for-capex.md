## Context

Layoffs because of AI have been happening for two years. What changed this quarter is the wording. Companies have stopped softening it. Oracle cut 30,000 in one announcement. Intuit cut 17% and said the reason was AI. Meta cut 8,000 — same reason. Cisco's CEO wrote a blog post saying the company was cutting people partly to spend more on AI. None of these companies are struggling. They're profitable. They're just moving the money. On the other side of that trade, big tech is set to spend around $700 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026. The two numbers are connected.

## Something Changed in the Last Three Months

Through the first six months of 2026, the tech industry has laid off around 142,000 people. That's about 852 jobs gone every day. Oracle alone cut 30,000 in one announcement — the biggest single layoff of the year. The Skillsyncer tracker, updated daily, confirms the totals. Numbers this big in a year that isn't a recession are unusual on their own. What's more unusual is what's being said about them.

Last month, Intuit cut 17% of its workforce — around 3,000 people. The reason they gave was AI. Meta laid off 8,000 in the same window. Same reason. Cisco's CEO, Chuck Robbins, wrote a blog post saying the headcount cuts were partly to spend more on AI tools for the people still there. Three announcements. Same week. Same pattern. The language stopped being 'difficult market conditions' or 'streamlining for the future.' It became closer to 'we're moving the money.' These are not struggling companies. Oracle, Intuit, Meta, and Cisco are all profitable. They're just choosing where the next dollar goes.

On the other side of that choice, the AI companies are getting funded at numbers that match. Big tech is set to spend around $700 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026. Apollo and Blackstone just put together a $36 billion deal in May to buy Google TPU chips for Anthropic — the biggest chip-financing deal in history. Anthropic itself closed a funding round at a $965 billion valuation, the first AI company to be worth more than OpenAI on paper. The products shipped the same month: Claude Opus 4.8 from Anthropic on June 2, Microsoft's first in-house coding model at Build on June 2-3, Google's new Gemini at I/O. Gartner described the enterprise AI coding agent market on May 20 as entering an 'expansion and competitive realignment' phase. Translation: the tools the laid-off people used to compete with are now in real production.

CBS News pointed out something else worth noticing in their June 1 piece. The headline layoffs are real. But the bigger long-term effect on workers is probably in hiring, not firing — and especially in junior and entry-level roles. The work AI does best right now is also the work new people used to do to learn the job. Companies aren't replacing junior roles as people leave. They're just not opening them again.

## What Actually Changed — In Plain English

For two years, companies that laid off people because of AI used softer words for it. 'Difficult decisions.' 'Streamlining operations.' 'A challenging environment.' This quarter, the softening stopped. Oracle, Intuit, Meta, Cisco — they're just saying it. We're moving the money. The honesty is the news. Because once one CEO says it plainly, every other CEO has cover to say it too. The next four quarters of earnings calls are going to sound different than the last four.

The usual way to think about this is: AI does the task → tasks make up jobs → jobs go away. That's true, but it leaves out the most important part. The companies are also choosing where to spend their money. They used to spend it on people. They're now spending it on AI. The AI does the work. The money flows back to AI companies when these same enterprises pay subscription fees. The whole thing is a deliberate choice that's now visible on three or four corporate balance sheets. Not a robot showing up uninvited.

For anyone running an HR, finance, or operations function inside a smaller company, the question used to be: will AI affect our team? That has an answer now. Yes. The new question is harder: how many people do we actually need for the work we do, given what AI can now do? That number is going to be a real conversation in budget meetings this fall. The companies that figure out their answer first will be running on a very different cost structure by 2027. The ones that wait will be answering the question with a layoff instead of a plan.

The part of this that should worry anyone hiring is the entry-level rung. The work AI is best at right now is the work we used to give to new hires to teach them the job. If the company doesn't open junior roles, where do the next generation of senior people come from? The capex side of this story has a funding plan. The 'how do junior people grow into the senior roles we'll need in 2030' side does not. That's the part of the conversation that hasn't started yet. It probably should.
