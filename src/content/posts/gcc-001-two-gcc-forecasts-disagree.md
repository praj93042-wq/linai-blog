## Context

Seven Indian states are now bidding for Global Capability Centres with formal policy, and the union government is finalising a national framework to sit above them. In the same week, PwC India and FICCI estimated that skill gaps place 19.32% of India's GCC sector value at risk by 2030. Those two developments belong in the same sentence more often than they appear in one.

## Policy moved on three levels at once

India is finalising its first National GCC Policy, a layer above the state schemes that have driven the last two years. Haryana released its GCC Policy 2026, joining the queue. Karnataka's labour code rules are pending notification, which will change day-one compliance for every Bengaluru centre. CBRE's new analysis finds seven states collectively targeting 1,380 new GCCs and 1.18 million jobs by 2031.

State targets are political commitments, not forecasts. They describe what governments have promised, which is a different thing from what the labour market can supply.

Three centres opened in the same week, and all three chose Hyderabad. Charles Schwab opened its first India technology capability centre there, saying it will reach 2,000 people by 2027. Proofpoint opened an AI centre with plans for 200 engineers. Acumatica opened a GCC. Bengaluru, which holds roughly 36 to 40% of India's GCC talent, took none of them.

## A caution about the forecasts themselves

Two of the largest real estate advisories published 2030 projections six months apart, from the same base. Zinnov and nasscom put India at 2,117 GCCs in FY26. JLL, in February, forecast over 2,500 GCCs by 2030. CBRE, this month, forecast 4,400. One implies roughly 380 net new centres. The other implies about 2,280.

That is not a rounding difference. It is the difference between a maturing market and a doubling one, and those call for different workforce plans.

There is a plausible reconciliation. The same Zinnov-nasscom data counts 2,117 centres across 3,728 units. Move 3,728 to 4,400 and you get roughly 18% growth, close to what JLL's centre-level figure implies. The two may be measuring different objects and broadly agreeing.

This is a hypothesis, not a finding. Confirming it requires both full reports, and we have obtained neither. We flag it rather than resolve it, because a projection that sets hiring assumptions should be understood before it is used.

For operators, three things follow. Treat state job targets as ambition, useful for reading where incentives land rather than for sizing a talent pool. Ask what a projection counts, because centres and units are not the same object. And watch the national policy: if it standardises incentives, the state-by-state arbitrage that currently shapes site selection weakens, and the decision returns to talent depth and cost.
