## Context

The headline numbers pull in opposite directions. Goldman Sachs estimates 300 million jobs globally are exposed to AI automation. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 projects 92 million roles displaced by 2030 — but also 170 million new ones created. Net positive on paper. But the data beneath the headline tells a more complicated story: who loses, who gains, and how fast the transition is moving.

## The Displacement Is Already Happening

The aggregate numbers obscure where the pressure is landing. It is not on factory floors. It is on white-collar workers earning up to $80,000 a year — the segment researchers from the University of Pennsylvania and OpenAI identified as most exposed to language model automation. In the first six months of 2025 alone, 77,999 tech job losses were directly attributed to AI. Unemployment among 20-to-30-year-olds in tech-exposed occupations has risen by almost 3 percentage points since early 2025 — a cohort-specific shock that aggregate employment data obscures.

The entry-level pipeline is the clearest signal. Stanford Digital Economy Lab data shows that hiring in AI-exposed entry-level roles has dropped 13% since large language models began proliferating. Workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations have seen a 13% employment decline since 2022. The traditional career ladder — enter at the bottom, learn on the job, move up — is being disrupted at the first rung. Junior analysts, junior copywriters, junior coders, junior paralegals: these are the roles disappearing first. Not because the work is gone, but because AI can do the routine components that used to justify the headcount.

Companies are not announcing layoffs. They are simply not backfilling. McKinsey's 2025 workplace survey found that 32% of organizations expect workforce decreases from AI, 43% expect no change, and only 13% expect increases. The mechanism is attrition, not termination: marketing teams, call centres, and coding departments running smaller as positions go unfilled. The World Economic Forum put numbers on this: 50% of business leaders already report 10-20% overcapacity in their organisations, primarily due to automation. By 2028, 40% expect 30-39% excess capacity. Structural redundancy is not coming. For many organisations, it is already here.

## The Paradox at the Centre

The pace distinguishes this from prior technology transitions. The WEF estimates AI is displacing approximately 1 million jobs per year. Globalisation — the last comparable structural shock to labour markets — displaced roughly 180,000 jobs per year at its peak. AI is moving 5x faster. The adjustment mechanisms that absorbed globalisation's disruption — geographic mobility, retraining programmes, sector shifts — were built for a slower rate of change.

Yet the net employment forecast remains positive, and not all economists accept the displacement narrative. Apollo Global's chief economist Torsten Slok invokes the Jevons Paradox: when a technology makes a task cheaper to perform, demand for that task tends to rise, often expanding total employment rather than contracting it. Steam engines did not reduce the number of workers needed for transport — they expanded it by orders of magnitude. The printing press did not eliminate scribes' livelihoods across the economy — it created an entirely new publishing industry. The historical pattern for general purpose technologies is net job creation, not destruction.

The data is consistent with both interpretations simultaneously. Displacement is real and concentrated. Net creation is likely and diffuse. The workers who lose a junior analyst role in 2025 are not automatically the workers who fill an AI trainer role in 2027. The gap between them — in skills, geography, time — is where the genuine policy and business challenge sits.

## What the Skills Gap Data Tells Us

The wage divergence is already visible. PwC's 2025 Global AI Jobs Barometer found that workers with AI skills now command salaries 56% higher than peers in identical roles without those skills. This is the largest skill premium PwC has recorded for any technology in its barometer's history. Meanwhile, 94% of business leaders report AI-critical skill shortages today — with one in three reporting gaps of 40% or more. The same organisations reporting overcapacity from automation are simultaneously reporting they cannot find people with the skills to build and manage the systems causing that overcapacity.

The implication is not that AI eliminates work. It is that AI is bifurcating the labour market faster than reskilling infrastructure can respond. The 300 million jobs exposed to automation are not all going to disappear. Most will be transformed — some tasks automated, others augmented, the remaining reconfigured. The workers who navigate that transformation and acquire AI-adjacent skills will likely earn more than their predecessors. The workers who do not will face a labour market that has moved on without them. The data does not tell us which group will be larger. It tells us the gap between them is growing, and it is growing fast.
