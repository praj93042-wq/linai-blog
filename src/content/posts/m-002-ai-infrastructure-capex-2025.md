## Context

Global AI infrastructure spending hit $318 billion in 2025, more than doubling 2024's $153 billion. Hyperscalers are projected to deploy over $600 billion in capex in 2026 alone. But JPMorgan estimates $650 billion in annual revenue is needed in perpetuity for a 10% return — and 95% of enterprises still report no measurable AI ROI.

## The Numbers Behind the Build-Out

The five hyperscalers — Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta, and Oracle — are the engine. Goldman Sachs Research puts combined hyperscaler capex at $342 billion in 2025, a 62% increase year-over-year. For 2026, the consensus has moved from $500 billion to over $600 billion, with Amazon alone projecting $200 billion, Alphabet at $175–185 billion, Meta at $115–135 billion, and Microsoft tracking above $120 billion. Wall Street analysts now project Big Tech capex exceeding $1 trillion in 2027.

Goldman Sachs' baseline model extends that trajectory further: $765 billion in annual AI capex in 2026, growing to $1.6 trillion by 2031. Combined hyperscaler spend from 2025 through 2027 is projected to reach $1.15 trillion — more than double the $477 billion deployed in the three years prior. Every year, analyst consensus has underestimated actual AI spend by more than 30 percent.

Geographically, the United States accounts for 77% of all global AI infrastructure spending — $69.2 billion in Q4 2025 alone, up 81.5% year-over-year. China tells a different story: AI infrastructure investment declined 8.1% year-over-year, reflecting the impact of US export controls on advanced semiconductors. The most dramatic growth came from an unexpected region: Middle East and Africa was up 535% year-over-year, driven by sovereign AI investment programs in Saudi Arabia and the UAE. Western Europe grew 42%, Asia-Pacific (excluding Japan) 47%.

IDC forecasts total AI infrastructure spend reaching $487 billion in 2026 and surpassing $1 trillion by 2029, implying a compound annual growth rate of approximately 31% through the rest of the decade. If that trajectory holds, the AI infrastructure market will be larger in 2029 than the entire global cloud computing market was in 2024.

## Who Is Spending It, Who Benefits, and Whether Anyone Earns It Back

The returns so far are real but uneven. Google Cloud revenue surged 63% year-over-year to $20 billion. Microsoft's AI business now runs at a $37 billion annualized revenue rate, up 123%. AWS posted its fastest growth in 15 quarters at 28%. The infrastructure is producing revenue — but the capex-to-revenue ratios are difficult to sustain. Meta is spending 54 cents of every dollar of revenue on capital investment. Microsoft 47 cents. Alphabet 46 cents. Amazon's free cash flow collapsed to $1.2 billion as infrastructure spending consumed nearly all operating cash flow.

Ferguson Wellman Capital Management identifies three layers of beneficiaries from this build-out. First, the Builders — the hyperscalers themselves, who are both the primary investors and the primary revenue beneficiaries, but are running capex ratios that compress near-term earnings. Second, the Enablers — semiconductor manufacturers, GPU producers, power utilities, electrical equipment makers, and construction firms facing capacity constraints through at least 2027. Third, the Applications layer — software companies integrating AI into products — where the long-term value likely accrues, but the investment case remains unclear as the winners have not yet separated from the field.

JPMorgan has put the clearest number on the return problem: $650 billion in annual revenue would need to be generated 'into perpetuity' to deliver a 10% return on current AI infrastructure investment. The bank drew an explicit parallel to the late-1990s telecom fiber buildout, which ultimately produced enormous infrastructure but destroyed capital at the investor level before the internet finally absorbed the capacity. KKR offers the counterpoint: 'Yes, there's froth. Yes, there will be a shake-out. History shows that technological revolutions often overshoot in the short term but compound in the long term.'

At the enterprise level, the disconnect is stark. MIT Media Lab research found that 95% of organisations see no measurable returns from AI adoption. Deloitte's 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise found that only 20% of companies report revenue growth attributable to AI. PwC's 2026 Global CEO Survey found that 56% say they have gotten 'nothing out of' their AI investments. McKinsey's position is that the productivity payoff is real but highly conditional — dependent on implementation quality, workforce re-skilling, and process redesign, not just model deployment.

The central tension in the AI infrastructure story is between the certainty of the capital commitment and the uncertainty of its absorption. The hyperscalers are spending as if demand will materialize at scale. They are not wrong to bet on it — cloud, mobile, and broadband all eventually absorbed their infrastructure overbuild. The question is timing, and whether today's investors are priced for the compound or for the shake-out. The data does not resolve that question. But it makes the stakes precise: $318 billion in 2025. $600 billion in 2026. One trillion in 2029. The build-out is not slowing down.
